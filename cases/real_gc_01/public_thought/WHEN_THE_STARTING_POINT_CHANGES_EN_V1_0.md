# When the Starting Point of Negotiation Changes

## A Thought Experiment on Deep U.S.–China Cooperation in the Age of AGI

By Zijunfu (子君赋) | Produced by Civilization Leap Research Group (文明跃迁研究组)

REAL GC 01 | Public thought experiment | English edition V1.0 | 25 September 2026

> Not first deciding, separately, how to win and then asking whether to cooperate—but deciding anew what is worth winning within humanity’s shared future, a future that includes ourselves.

## 1. Begin with someone who is not at the table

Imagine a storm that has not yet happened.

In a coastal region with limited resources, a mother hears a broadcast but cannot tell whether the places it names include her village. A hospital receives a warning but does not know whether vehicles will arrive to evacuate its patients. A meteorologist sees two advanced models disagree, with no way to tell why.

Meanwhile, negotiators from the United States and China are discussing cooperation on artificial intelligence.

On the table are computing power, data, markets and intellectual property, as well as genuine security concerns on both sides. In what capacity will those absent people appear? As potential customers, costs of cooperation, factors affecting reputation—or people entitled to influence what the cooperation is for?

This time, change only that starting point.

*The scene, dialogue and proposed clauses are constructed for this thought experiment, not taken from meeting records. Future AGI capabilities are assumptions, not a report of capabilities already achieved.*

There is a real opening for the question. China’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs, in its account of the meeting on 24 September 2026, reports a new joint economic and trade arrangement and discussion of dialogue on AI’s risks and benefits. Its account includes remarks by both sides about AI cooperation, but it is not an implemented agreement on disaster early warnings.[1] A pre-summit USTR statement likewise refers both to mutually beneficial trade and to the interests of American farmers, manufacturers and workers.[2]

The question here is specific: could the two countries move AI cooperation on disaster warnings beyond an exchange of technology and market access, toward jointly creating the capacity to keep people safe?

This does not invent international cooperation from scratch. The Early Warnings for All initiative, advanced by the World Meteorological Organization and its partners, already connects risk knowledge, monitoring and forecasting, warning communication, and preparedness and response.[3] The question is not who first thought of saving lives. It is what, when interests conflict, can justify reopening goals that have already been set.

## 2. Give both starting points the same information

Suppose both sides receive exactly the same information. Each has strengths in observations, models and practical applications. Combining information may improve warnings, but no improvement is guaranteed. Some places do not need another forecast map as much as they need understandable messages, reliable communications and the ability to act. Poorly designed sharing can create security risks and dependency. Budgets are finite. Businesses need reasonable returns. Local institutions do not want to become subordinate users who cannot leave.

Both versions of the thought experiment also have the same AGI assistance: the ability to connect existing information, explore interactions among hazards, communications, transport, healthcare and supply chains, generate different arrangements, and explain uncertainty and disagreement. It cannot read minds, invent missing facts or make commitments on anyone’s behalf.

Keep budgets, security limits and negotiating time the same. Do not give the second table extra money, a better model or a perfectly trustworthy counterpart.

This imagined capability has a foothold in present practice, but the two are not equivalent. WMO describes AI applications in forecasting, impact assessment and communication, while emphasizing the continuing importance of data, physical science, operational infrastructure and human expertise.[4]

Both tables can be farsighted. Both can consider reputation, reciprocity, violations of agreements and consequences that return to the initiator. We do not portray “self-interest first” as stupidity, or assign the real United States and China to opposing moral categories.

The actual difference lies in one question:

**Must the circumstances of others first be translated into “useful to us” before they can count as a reason to change our goals?**

## 3. The first table: how can cooperation better serve our goals?

In the first version, each side begins by defining its own tasks: protect its public, strengthen its technological and industrial capabilities, secure sustainable returns and avoid unacceptable security exposure. A joint arrangement must first show how it helps fulfill those tasks.

This table is not destined to fail.

The U.S. side might offer model services, equipment and market opportunities. The Chinese side might offer observational cooperation, practical settings for deployment and joint research. Either could advocate open basic warnings. Either might support regions with limited resources. A negotiator skilled at pursuing long-term interests can certainly see value in helping others.

Here, AGI can trace the consequences further. Closed interfaces may lead to duplicated systems. Exclusive markets may encourage rival coalitions. Turning warnings into bargaining chips may damage credibility. It can also find reciprocal arrangements better than the parties’ original offers.

Yet, so long as one’s own interests retain final priority, discussion can narrow at a particular point.

Suppose the shared information shows that the project can already serve both countries and paying markets. Extending basic service to a region with little purchasing power would require shifting part of the promotional budget and forgoing some exclusive revenue. There might be a long-term return, but no reliable evidence currently establishes it. Nobody can promise that the extension will ultimately improve either provider’s finances.

One response consistent with the first rule would be:

> “We recognize the need. First explain how it serves our agreed objectives, or who will meet the additional cost. This as-yet-unfunded need should not change the cooperation already arranged.”

The region has not been declared undeserving. Its needs have simply not acquired standing as a reason to change the main agreement. Aid may be discussed separately. The project may expand later. For now, the order of priorities remains.

The issue is not whether the parties understand consequences. It is which consequences must be accepted as reasons to revise the purpose itself.

If this table ultimately includes the region, record that honestly. Perhaps it identified a sufficient long-term return. Perhaps an existing public obligation directly constrained the choice. Perhaps the essay has misunderstood its starting point. Do not remove cooperation from the first column to manufacture a contrast.

## 4. The second table: first decide what is worth accomplishing together

The second version does not require either side to make a unilateral concession first. Both arrive with the same information, resources and limits. What changes is the order in which they form their tasks.

They first ask: what capacity should we create together to address harm we can already foresee but may still fail to prevent? How can our own side develop safely within that undertaking and earn reasonable returns?

Here, the widest circle of “others” is humanity as a whole—including the United States and China. Humanity is not a third country sitting beside them, nor a total score obtained by adding everyone’s gains and losses. Neither country’s development stands outside humanity’s shared future. Nor can that whole be invoked to erase the circumstances of particular people.

Local meteorologists, hospitals, communications providers, workers and residents need not first become major customers or strategic partners for their circumstances to shape the task.

Consider this constructed exchange:

> **Local representative:** “It is not that we have never received a forecast. We need to know whether it applies here, whether the message can reach people, and where to turn when something is wrong.”
>
> **Chinese representative:** “Then providing a forecast is not the end of this cooperation.”
>
> **U.S. representative:** “Business revenues and technology security still have to be addressed. But we can distinguish basic warning needs from value-added services, rather than make them a single condition of access.”
>
> **Joint chair:** “First define the capabilities people need. Then discuss how each party can provide them, share the costs and benefit.”

AGI has not declared what justice requires. It helps reveal that better model accuracy, cheaper translation, offline communication and improved hospital response may be different links in the same problem. Spending the entire budget on a leading performance metric may not best fill the missing capability.

Within the same budget, the parties can now compare different arrangements. Could fewer duplicated demonstrations pay for local adaptation? Could minimally necessary open interfaces preserve commercial services while reducing dependency? Would keeping two independent models and making their disagreements visible be more reliable than merging them into one apparently more efficient system?

None of this is free. Whether it is worthwhile still requires judgment. But those affected are no longer merely objects of assessment after the fact. They can change the task the parties undertake.

## 5. Write the changed position into a different clause

Both tables may write “strengthen AI cooperation.” The difference must appear in what follows.

Under the first set of goals, a proposed clause might read:

> Subject to technology-security and intellectual-property protections, the parties will exchange specified forecasting products, improve warning services in their own countries and agreed markets, and negotiate wider coverage on the basis of demonstrable reciprocal benefits.

Under the second, a different clause might read:

> The parties and participating regions will jointly define basic warning needs. Within mutually accepted resource and security limits, their shared task will be to make warnings usable, enable action and allow errors to be corrected. Commercial returns, contributions and technological competition will be organized around that task. Lack of purchasing power or equivalent technology will not, by itself, be grounds for excluding a basic warning need.

The first is not a bad agreement. The second will not become effective merely because it has been written. The visible difference is that the first makes existing national tasks the basis for expansion; the second lets shared needs shape the task itself—and, through that, reorder what each side seeks.

The same party’s positions might change as follows:

| Negotiating question | When one’s own interests have priority in setting goals | When humanity and those affected help shape the goals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What do we want to win? | More markets, technological returns and relative advantage, while reducing our own risks through cooperation. | Greater creativity, reliability and reasonable returns in solving shared problems, without making others’ dependency a condition of success. |
| What do we exchange? | Primarily the reciprocal value of data, technology, markets and commitments. | First identify needed capabilities; then combine contributions, licenses, services and funding. Not everyone must offer the same kind of leverage. |
| Who can change coverage? | Assess requests against our goals, reciprocal offers, existing obligations and expected returns. | Those who bear consequences can raise needs and objections. Having little leverage does not remove their standing to influence the agenda. |
| What kind of lead is worth retaining? | Exclusive interfaces or influence over the counterpart’s access may remain bargaining advantages. | Retain competition in products, algorithms and services, while asking whether exclusivity obstructs the basic task or lasting local capability. |
| What about uncertain costs to others? | Decide whether to investigate, reserve judgment or defer, according to our risks and obligations. | A major unknown can itself justify joint inquiry, limited steps and correction, without proof that the harm will rebound on us. |
| What remains if relations deteriorate? | Reassess cooperation according to our returns, security and commitments. | Security review may still tighten, but handle minimally necessary, verified life-saving information separately from unrelated commercial disputes. |

These are positions that different rules may generate, not predictions of national behavior. The second column of positions is not a catalogue of inventions that the first could never conceive.

What changes may be not only the available plans, but the purposes. What leaves the contest may be not a country, but a kind of victory no longer worth pursuing.

## 6. The second starting point must face hard questions too

### When the other party can neither repay nor retaliate

If the story ends with mutual benefit, farsighted self-interest may reach the same destination. To see the difference, leave the uncomfortable conditions in place.

Assume explicitly that including the resource-limited region adds an affordable cost and reduces some exclusive revenue. It does not endanger either provider’s basic security, but there is no demonstrable net return. Do not quietly insert “reputation will eventually pay” to make the difficulty disappear.

If additional gains for one’s own side retain final priority, not yet including the region may fit the first rule. Under the second, the absence of a return cannot, by itself, dismiss those people’s basic needs. The parties must reconsider tasks and spending, seek a sustainable way to extend coverage, and explain to their own publics what is being undertaken and why it is worthwhile.

This does not prove that both parties are financially better off. It recognizes what can legitimately lead both to reconsider the meaning of “better.”

Self-interest has not been abolished. Providers must remain viable, meet responsibilities at home and account for their use of resources. **Self-interest with others first is not an unlimited obligation to pay, nor a claim to moral superiority purchased by taking basic protection away from vulnerable people at home.**

### When cooperation itself increases risk

Suppose the parties propose concentrating all forecasts, data and publication authority in one shared platform. New information suggests that this could create common failures, data misuse or a single authority that local users cannot challenge.

The second starting point should permit rejection of this “deeper cooperation” in favor of limited interoperability, multiple models and independent checks. Counterexamples must not be silenced in cooperation’s name.

Here the set of options may shrink rather than grow. What disappears is an arrangement that disguises shared vulnerability as shared capability.

### When one party keeps the old priority

One side can use the new starting point to examine its own goals. It cannot commit the other side. Unilateral adoption neither guarantees agreement nor requires handing over sensitive capabilities that cannot be recovered.

It can propose services it can provide without the counterpart’s cooperation, or work with other willing participants. Jointly funded activities still require clear commitments, authority and conditions for withdrawal. If the counterpart exploits an arrangement to cause danger, the dangerous part can be suspended. Commitment to cooperation must not become denial of risk.

### When “for humanity” overrides local voices

A region with limited resources may reject the proposal. It may refuse to share residents’ locations, reject evacuation decisions made by an external system, or decline long-term dependency in exchange for immediate service.

That is not an obstacle to be eliminated. It is a reason to redesign. The United States and China cannot define humanity’s needs in advance and then demand that particular people comply. Future generations cannot attend, but their interests should be considered through public reasons, alternative scenarios and contestable forms of representation—not by an AGI claiming to speak for them.

**Humanity is not a blank authorization. The wider our frame of reference, the less excuse it gives us to ignore particular people.**

## 7. What civilizational capacity is AGI amplifying?

Suppose future capabilities grow further. The same powers of modeling, simulation and organization can identify a counterpart’s weaknesses faster—or reveal tasks neither side had thought to request.

Under the new starting point, three uses are worth demonstrating.

First, connect different people’s difficulties. The forecast is already good enough, but the message does not arrive. The message arrives, but transport and medicines are unavailable. People act, but errors on the ground cannot be fed back into the model. “Improve another leading metric” may then give way to “repair the break in the chain that puts lives at risk.”

Second, explore combinations previously difficult to consider together. Within the same resource limits, compare licensing, local training, commercial services, offline interfaces and checks by multiple parties. What expands may be not only the speed of calculation, but people’s ability to form a shared task.

Third, retain shared experience. When the next storm comes, there should be no need to rediscover who identified an error, which methods worked locally, how disagreements were explained or how technology was translated into action.

AGI itself guarantees none of this direction. Greater capability can amplify shared creation, exclusion or control. Seeing more is an opportunity. Allowing the people thereby made visible to influence the goals is a further choice.

The potential outcome of this negotiation is therefore more than a service contract. It may create a lasting sequence:

> Recognize problems together → form shared tasks → bring different strengths to bear → allow results to be challenged → retain useful capabilities in society.

As regions develop their own judgment and capacity to act, the United States and China need not acquire more securely dependent clients. They can gain more capable partners in creating something together. Contributions still have value. Returns still require negotiation. But “make others permanently unable to do without us” need not remain the only commercial or strategic future worth defending.

## 8. How CCM can put this difference in people’s hands

CCM—Computable Cooperation Mechanisms—need not begin by asking readers to install software. It can begin with a comparison they can understand and change: the same facts, examined from two starting points; how goals changed; and why a particular option remained.

The simplest use is to choose a real issue, state the shared facts and responsibilities no party can evade, let the parties and those affected express their needs, then use both rules to generate goals, positions and options. Finally, retain the conditions that make the preferred conclusion uncomfortable. If nothing changes, say so. If only distribution changes, do not claim that the purpose has changed.

This does not replace judgment with a table. It makes the origins of a judgment visible.

If someone cannot repay us, can that person still change our decision? If someone is absent from the negotiating table, can that person still influence its goals? When cooperation creates danger, do we allow it to be rejected?

No political declaration is needed. Finding a flaw in any one of these questions can advance the research. The existing CCM entry accepts read-only analysis, source checks and private feedback without prior acceptance of the wider theory.[5]

**The research takes a clear position: replacing self-interest first with self-interest that puts others first—as the underlying logic of human society and AGI goal formation—is the only path out of the cycle in which security is pursued by continually weakening others, whose countermeasures then provoke further escalation, and the only path to safeguarding a shared future.** The claim stands, and so does the invitation to refute it. This limited thought experiment is not presented as a final proof.

In particular, offer counterexamples showing how a different starting point, under the same capabilities and resources, low trust, unequal power and no demonstrable return, can still reliably give those affected and the shared future standing to change the goals. Also identify any coercion, dependency or needless sacrifice hidden in this essay’s second set of proposals. If it is there, the proposal must change. Renaming it to preserve the conclusion is not an answer.

The mother at the beginning need not know how many bargaining chips the two countries exchanged.

She needs to know whether the information concerns her, whether there is somewhere to seek clarification, and whether anyone is arranging help that can actually reach her.

**When negotiation allows these questions to shape its purpose, it is doing more than deciding who will get more from the world. It is deciding where our new capabilities will take that world.**
